## Reusable themes and family realities

These are candidates for canonical guidance shared across browsing ages, with
local readiness/context references where a specific action changes. They are not
already allocated or approved content records.

- **Observe, offer, adjust:** reusable across Play & discovery, Everyday care,
  and A home that helps (**PDF 33–37, 119–126**). Interest, manageable effort,
  frustration, tiredness, hunger, and overstimulation matter more than completing
  an invitation. For infant play, the adult restores materials after play; the
  book explicitly distinguishes this from expectations for older children
  (**PDF 121–122**).
- **Connection in ordinary care:** suitable for Everyday care and Connection &
  feelings (**PDF 99–100, 109–114, 125**). The connection checklist is a
  perspective on interactions, not a reason to turn every feeding, bath, or
  diaper change into another task to perform.
- **A familiar base with room to explore:** reusable home/relationship theme
  (**PDF 29–30, 108–110, 253–257**). Distinguish responsive caregiving from any
  particular sleep surface, feeding method, or childcare purchase.
- **The adult's capacity matters:** primary home in For you & your family
  (**PDF 235–242, 244–246**). The text names exhaustion, isolation, guilt from
  idealized online images, conflicting advice, and difficulty asking for help.
  Assistance with meals, errands, cleaning, or older children can support the
  adult's time with the baby; paid assistance is repeatedly qualified by access
  or means. Learning something unrelated to parenting is included.
- **Family forms are not exceptions to eligibility:** the introductory family
  framing explicitly includes different primary caregivers, equal co-parents,
  people who cannot or choose not to breastfeed, adoptive parents absent from
  gestation/early infancy, and early return to work (**PDF 41, opening paragraph**).
  Later passages include single parents, separated co-parents, distant family,
  and care shared outside the home (**PDF 244–247, 253–257**). Do not present a
  stay-at-home birth mother with nearby relatives as the default requirement.
- **Children within a family remain individuals:** twins' names and individual
  needs matter (**PDF 228**). Siblings may welcome participation or decline it;
  resentment can be heard while harmful actions are limited (**PDF 225–227**).
  Adult guilt about dividing attention is also part of the source. Do not turn
  older siblings into responsible caregivers through a shortened anecdote.
- **Support without denying difference:** the disability/neurodivergence passage
  emphasizes individual capability, different timelines, observation, and
  needed assistance; it explicitly says specialist detail exceeds the book's
  scope (**PDF 230**). Keep that limit visible. The adoption and prematurity
  sections contain specific medical/care suggestions that need their own review
  before any practical reuse (**PDF 229–230**).
- **Warm care within real constraints:** the childcare discussion recognizes
  availability and budget limitations, asks families to assess care as it
  currently is, and explicitly considers warm non-Montessori care as an option
  (**PDF 253–255**). Its detailed ideal checklist is not a universal eligibility
  test for a good caregiver.
- **Toddler continuity with changed demands:** the transition describes order,
  preferences, turn-taking, limits, strong feelings, participation, and increasing
  complexity (**PDF 260–262**). It also acknowledges mornings when a train or
  appointment makes rushing necessary (**PDF 262**). These pages are an overview;
  later toddler content should use the Toddler source and resolve overlap.

## Safety-sensitive editorial boundaries and source tensions

These are boundaries for faithful representation, not new medical advice or
external safety verification.

1. **Observation is qualified by safety and responsiveness.** The source limits
   freedom by safety/ability (**PDF 103**) and describes responding to needs
   promptly (**PDF 109**). Its crying passage qualifies an adult's brief step
   away with the baby not being in danger (**PDF 110**). Do not isolate
   “wait,” “do not interrupt,” or the visitor-note fall example from this context
   (**PDF 101, 107, 110–111, 126, 257–258**). These notes do not establish a
   crying, injury, or emergency response protocol.
2. **General philosophy cannot approve sleep, feeding, or movement setups.**
   Introductory/principle pages mention floor beds, infant positioning, swaddling,
   supports, food, and water (**PDF 18, 32–34, 105, 107–110, 120, 127,
   254–255**). Those mentions do not supply the full dedicated source context.
   Later practical entries and illustrations must read and cite their complete
   source sections and retain relevant cautions. No sleep/feeding safety review
   was performed here.
3. **Natural materials are not an unconditional safety assurance.** The authors'
   material advice includes finish/paint, object size, secured components, and
   choking considerations (**PDF 120**), and safe exploration qualifies the
   freedom to use objects (**PDF 121**). The same section proposes repurposing
   objects; do not carry attractive-material suggestions forward while dropping
   the checks. The dimensions and improvised test given on PDF 120 are source
   claims requiring their own precise review, not a certification by this project.
4. **Support remains available when a timeline differs.** Preserve the explicit
   support-seeking statement for developmental concerns (**PDF 119**) and the
   limited specialist scope in the differences passage (**PDF 230**). An
   observation principle does not justify telling a family that all concerns
   will resolve by waiting.
5. **Adult distress is not only a self-care exercise.** The authors direct readers
   struggling with postpartum depression toward a doctor or trusted health
   professional (**PDF 238**); the calm-ideas list again includes seeking help
   for depression/burnout (**PDF 241**). Do not replace that passage with
   gratitude or a rest suggestion alone. Its prevalence figure is a book claim,
   not independently verified here.
6. **Keep co-parenting's safety exception.** The wish for time with both parents
   explicitly excludes situations where the baby's safety is at stake
   (**PDF 247**). Do not universalize reconciliation or shared contact.
7. **Record, rather than erase, deterministic passages.** PDF 96 says basic trusts
   can only be given in the first year and supplies second-/ninth-month timings;
   PDF 97 and PDF 108–109 make broad lifelong-outcome claims. The book elsewhere
   stresses individual timelines and offers later family-bonding contexts
   (**PDF 119, 229–230, 238–242**). These claims need explicit disposition and
   source/tone review; a companion deadline or a guarantee is not justified by
   the foundation's general themes. The preparatory/pregnancy parts of these
   passages remain out of product scope.
8. **The source mixes philosophy with health and science assertions.** Examples
   encountered include a sleep total at **PDF 105**, a numerical processing
   interval at **PDF 102**, neurological claims about oral exploration at
   **PDF 222**, and a claim of healing effects from particular music frequencies
   at **PDF 241**. The “49 ideas” are introduced as things that helped the
   authors (**PDF 239**). Do not inherit all those statements as validated facts
   because adjacent suggestions are useful. A future disposition can retain a
   broader supported theme while explicitly accounting for the omitted claim.
9. **Author anecdotes are not general permissions.** Budget ideas on **PDF 224**,
   sibling-care anecdotes on **PDF 225–226**, and play-object examples on
   **PDF 126** need contextual safety review if converted into actions or art.
   An anecdote should remain marked as such in evidence even when paraphrased.
