Coverage: written explanations and captions, with selected stills; see the archive and media limits. The ten cue cards and long reading inventories can be expanded individually.

Start here: Clarke's most useful recurring idea is to make the parts of the stroke work together. A deeper, more spin-oriented swing needs room to accelerate; a compact redirection stroke can succeed with a flatter path. His writing supports several effective backhands, rather than one silhouette everyone should copy. This is our synthesis of his FAA analysis, Alcaraz update, and technique decision tree.

This is a research reference and experiment brief. The beeps below are our proposed tests, not drills Clarke has prescribed or features already implemented in Tennis Trainer. His explanations of professional players are coaching interpretations; they do not establish that changing one feature will cause the same result in recreational players.

For club players, start with the ball. Clarke explicitly favors learning to produce a useful ball for a tactical purpose, using constraints and feedback on racket path, angle and speed, over pursuing ideal form with repeated wrist/arm instructions. Use the technical map to choose an experiment that fixes a real shot problem. Keep a ball-target-only drill as the comparison: the beep should earn its place. Everything Since Wimbledon

Arm structures and hand emphasis

In The Two-Handed Backhand...Again, Clarke groups examples into top arm straight / bottom arm bent, both arms bent, and bottom arm straight. These are tendencies; one player can use several depending on the ball. He also revises his earlier top-hand-dominant mental model after experimenting with Karue Sell's bottom-hand cue. Test a compatible feel rather than assigning a fixed hand percentage. We cannot infer which hand supplied force from video.

For example, Djokovic Wins Gold contrasts two Alcaraz backhands: an earlier punch and a stretched, later hook. That within-player variation matters more than forcing every repetition into one professional's nominal category.

The 2025 Australian Open update also notes grip details such as Fonseca's grouped top-hand fingers versus Medvedev's spread index finger. These observations reinforce the need to record the player's actual grip. They do not establish an ideal finger spacing.

Equipment is part of his explanation

In the core essay, Clarke argues that racket mass distribution and resistance to twisting can contribute to stability, and contrasts Djokovic's and Nadal's equipment choices. Treat this as context, not a recommendation to copy a professional's specifications. His reported setups are historical and partly secondhand. Keep the player's equipment constant while comparing cues.

Important distinctions and changes in his thinking

  • Racket tip up is different from racket face up. Tip height describes where the racket head is; face angle describes the string bed. The upward-facing strings discussed in the 2023 ATP Finals analysis concern a particular follow-through. They are not a rule to open the face throughout the stroke.
  • Face orientation must be attached to a phase. The Ruud example praises more open strings in preparation. The Sinner example praises closure in the slot. “Open” and “closed” without an event, grip and reference plane are incomplete instructions.
  • Inside preparation is different from cramped contact. The 2022 FAA analysis praises bringing the racket farther back while criticizing inadequate elbow clearance at impact. A cue saying only “keep the elbow close” loses that phase distinction.
  • The initial setup does not determine the entire swing. In the Toronto analysis, Sinner starts outside but creates much more space through the subsequent turn. Classifying an outside first frame as an error would misread the example.
  • Rolling can fit the whole stroke. The Fonseca analysis praises top-hand rolling with a longer inside path; the Sinner Miami analysis connects a deep path and closed strings with strong top-hand use, including against low slices. A short, steep, crowded swing is a different combination.
  • His early control preference becomes more qualified. Chasing Control is skeptical of spin-oriented, complicated actions; Top Strokes explains how Sinner/Rune generate substantial speed through their longer dynamic paths. These descriptions should remain attached to their dates and contexts.
  • Preparation height is adaptable. Clarke revisits his pessimism about Alcaraz's shortened backhand in the 2025 Roland Garros recap. His June update favors Alcaraz's compromise of lower hands with some restored racket-head height. FAA in 2026 and Wimbledon further emphasize reducing vertical amplitude under faster conditions.
  • Low preparation need not mean a short swing. The Cincinnati 2025 preview contrasts Sinner's low-but-long preparation with Alcaraz's more compact version. Vertical height and backward acceleration space are separate dimensions. Fonseca on grass and Alcaraz later that year show adjustments to preparation size.
  • The finish is diagnostic, not a universal target. The December 2025 mailbag explains why a linear drive often finishes higher and why other contacts need different finishes. A beep for “finish above your head” could reward an appearance without changing the strike.
  • No verified “drop as late as possible” rule for the two-hander. The late-drop passage found in the Paris 2026 recap concerns Shelton's forehand. Drop timing remains an exploratory measurement below, not a Clarke-endorsed optimization target.
  • Spin margin and timing margin differ. More topspin can help the ball clear the net and land in, while the motion used to create it can be harder to time. That is a tradeoff in his 2022 comparison, not a reason to eliminate topspin.
  • Direction changes the job. The Vienna 2023 analysis associates the stable-wrist drive with a longer directional window down the line, while rolling can help sharper crosscourt angles. Compare like-intended shots when testing C4.
  • A player can change the solution within a match. At Wimbledon and Vienna in 2025, Sinner uses a more compact, upright finish for particular precision shots as well as his familiar rolling pattern. Do not classify every Sinner-style swing as needing the same wrist action.
  • Ready includes the grip change. The 2024 Australian Open analysis discusses the time cost of Medvedev's unusual grip transition. A small backswing alone cannot establish readiness for a fast return.

First shortlist: C1, C2 and C3, choosing whichever addresses an observed issue in the player's stroke. This ranking is our judgment about relevance, interpretability and test value; it does not rank implementation cost or claim proven effectiveness. C9 and C10 belong to separate drills. Do not make a player chase all ten technical targets. Compare each against the same ball-flight goal without body instructions or sound.

M — milestone: “that movement just happened.” F — feedback: “that completed repetition matched the selected reference.” P — prompt: “start the action now.” The same sound must have only one meaning during a block. Shared definitions for the cue cards

Calibrate on five coach-accepted repetitions for one player, camera position, stroke intention and feed type. Draw the relevant positional corridors and label the movement events in video. Use body-scaled distances and equal-scale image units, using the oriented frame's width and height; do not treat independently normalized image x/y as equal units. A single camera supplies projected proxies unless geometry is independently established. A second view is useful for checking apparent contact clearance and inside/outside position.

Each card's “band” means the observed range in those reference repetitions, expanded only by measured annotation/tracking uncertainty. It is a pilot setting, not an ideal professional angle. If good and poor examples overlap within that uncertainty, the feature is not yet a useful discriminator.

Arm cues only during the selected two-handed stroke phase. Require a continuous, confidently observed track; suppress on missing landmarks, identity changes, ambiguous contact, or frame gaps that obscure the event. Confirm crossings across consecutive accepted observations, record the resulting delay, emit once per repetition, and rearm only after return to ready and the next feed. Silence means no judgment when observation is insufficient. It must not imply “bad technique.”

C1Room for the forward swingMovement marker

Rationale: Clarke repeatedly connects a fuller inside backswing with room to accelerate; the appropriateness of that path depends on the rest of the stroke. Firenze, Madrid

Sound: M at the first entry into the coach-marked slot corridor during the preparation-to-forward-swing transition. Check both the hand path relative to the torso and the racket head relative to the hands. The marker acknowledges entry only; score success afterward only if the subsequent path also travels toward the intended contact corridor. “Tip points backward at any time” is insufficient.

Observe: Both arms, torso reference and racket landmarks; a rear-oblique view that separates hands and racket, checked against a second view. Existing wrists alone give only a partial proxy. Do not infer actual inside-ball geometry from image overlap.

Suppress / pitfalls: Slice, return block, occluded racket, wrong swing phase, exaggerated backswing taken solely to earn the beep, or a compact stroke intentionally selected for redirection.

Trial / keep if: Compare cue-on/off rally drives. Keep if independent video review shows usable forward-swing space and directional consistency improves or holds without making the player late. Do not reward maximum backswing length.

C2Space at contactPost-stroke feedback

Rationale: Preparation close to the torso and clearance at contact serve different purposes. FAA

Some effective open-body, bottom-hand-led patterns appear crowded from behind. Judge a compatible player-specific relationship, not maximum separation. Backhand…Again

Sound: F after the stroke when top-elbow and hand clearance at labeled contact fall within the player's accepted range. Judge contact in a short labeled window; do not select whichever nearby frame looks best.

Observe: Both shoulders/elbows/wrists, torso position and verified contact timing. Start with side/rear-oblique video and a second view to reject false clearance caused by perspective. Current ball–wrist overlap does not identify impact.

Suppress / pitfalls: Jammed or emergency balls, out-of-profile feed height, arm occlusion, uncertain impact, or reaching excessively away from the body to satisfy the distance rule.

Trial / keep if: Use comparable waist-height feeds and a fixed target. Keep if cramped contacts decrease on blinded video review, without increasing reaching, mishits or target dispersion. This is post-stroke feedback, not a last-instant instruction to move the arms.

C3Drive through the strikeMovement marker

Rationale: Clarke contrasts successful arm structures and criticizes combinations that remain short while rolling; extension is conditional on the stroke. Barcelona, Montreal

Sound: M when the hands cross the selected forward boundary and the top arm reaches its individualized extension range, from late forward swing into early follow-through. Evaluate continued travel through the corridor afterward. The arm need not become straight; the marker alone does not certify a good strike.

Observe: Both arms, forward direction and phase; side-oblique video. A shoulder–elbow–wrist angle is an elbow-angle proxy, not a wrist-angle measurement. Check extension together with hand travel so an elbow flick alone does not qualify.

Suppress / pitfalls: Reaching before the swing, stretching after a miss, late/wide defensive replies, disappearance of an arm, or forcibly locking the elbow. Do not turn this into a high-finish requirement.

Trial / keep if: Hit moderate-spin drives to a fixed depth zone. Keep if through-contact travel improves and usable depth/direction holds or improves. Assess what happens before and around contact; a prettier finish alone is insufficient.

C4Controlled hand and racket actionPost-stroke feedback

Rationale: Clarke links stable contact relationships to redirection in flatter backhands, while recognizing other effective patterns. Korda/Djokovic, Fritz

Sound: F after a verified contact window matches the player's selected control-oriented pattern. Evaluate two separate features: hand orientation relative to the forearm, and racket-face orientation relative to its path. Report which feature was assessed.

Observe: Close, sufficiently fast hand/racket imagery or additional sensing. Wrist-center coordinates cannot measure wrist bend; a racket shaft line cannot establish the string-bed plane. Use reference trajectories, not zero change in world-space face angle.

Suppress / pitfalls: Motion blur, edge-on face ambiguity, missing hand landmarks, intentional heavy-spin patterns, emergency contact, or deliberate stiffening to “freeze” the racket. No visual claim about grip pressure or muscle tension.

Trial / keep if: Alternate agreed crosscourt/line targets in separate blocks. Keep if unnecessary contact-window manipulation decreases and directional dispersion improves, without losing useful depth. Highest sensing burden among the initial candidates.

Keep sharp-angle/heavy-spin intentions outside this initial comparison; their useful face trajectories can differ. Vienna

C5Racket power positionMovement marker

Rationale: Racket-head height can create useful preparation, but recent examples favor adapting that height to time available. Rune, Alcaraz update

Sound: M when racket-head height above the hand midpoint first enters the selected preparation band, before the forward swing. Check shaft direction against the reference as a separate condition. Relative head-to-hand height avoids rewarding a whole-body lift by itself.

Observe: Head/handle landmarks and both hands from a side-oblique view with the full racket in frame. Body pose alone is insufficient.

Suppress / pitfalls: Follow-through mistaken for preparation, raising the hands instead of establishing the intended racket relationship, crop/blur, fast returns, and high feeds outside the calibration group.

Trial / keep if: Use slower feeds requiring the player to create pace. Keep if preparation becomes repeatable and useful depth improves without late contact. Repeat with faster feeds before extending the cue's scope. The target is an appropriate band, never maximum height.

C6Preparation timingMovement marker

Rationale: Earlier preparation is specifically discussed by Konfederak in Clarke's Cerundolo interview. The reference deadline below is our experiment, not his numerical rule.

Sound: M when the hands and shoulder configuration reach the selected preparation checkpoint. Record its source-time interval to bounce and contact when those events are independently established. Do not assume “complete before bounce” suits every feed.

Observe: Both arms and shoulders from a stable rear-oblique view; incoming-ball observations for relative timing. Shoulder-line projection is not a direct measure of torso rotation.

Optional separate P trial: In predictable feeds only, a coach/feeder sends an early “begin preparing” prompt. Measure cue-to-action timing. This prompt is independent of the observed completion beep and is tested in another block.

Suppress / pitfalls: Holding a pose through multiple feeds, footwork turning the whole player without useful preparation, or a lost ball track used to invent a deadline.

Trial / keep if: Preparation timing becomes less rushed without prolonged waiting or worse contact. This is among the closest candidates to current landmarks, but the event detector still needs development.

C7Drop depth for this shotPost-stroke feedback

Rationale: Depth must fit the available swing length, face and intended trajectory. Dubai, Mail Bag 2026

Sound: F after the observed minimum racket-head height and subsequent approach match the selected drive or topspin profile. Express depth relative to the estimated contact-ball height, not the ball's unrelated height at the instant the racket is lowest.

Observe: Racket trajectory, ball/contact estimate and hands, preferably from the side with geometry checked. A temporal minimum needs later evidence; its beep cannot arrive at the minimum itself without prediction.

Suppress / pitfalls: Uncertain contact height, one-frame tracking dips, frames missing near the minimum, slices, low defensive balls, jumping contacts outside the calibrated drill, and comparing different incoming-ball heights in one reference band.

Trial / keep if: Test drive and heavier-topspin blocks separately. Keep if the intended trajectory/depth improves while contact consistency holds. Net clearance alone does not prove increased spin. Avoid “deeper always earns the beep.”

C8Hear the drop timingMovement marker · exploratory

Rationale: Clarke describes several sequencing patterns, including continued backward racket movement as the body moves forward. He does not establish a universal latest-drop target. ATP Finals, Top strokes

Sound: M when the racket head begins sustained descent after the selected preparation phase. Log that onset relative to forward-hand motion and contact. Descent must exceed the measured stationary tracking-noise band and persist across observations; timestamp the first qualifying descent, while retaining the later confirmation time.

Observe: Racket head, hands and body reference from a side-oblique view. Wrist height is not racket-head height. Body lowering can create apparent head descent, so examine both source-space and hand-relative movement.

Suppress / pitfalls: Tracking jitter, multiple unclassified loops, drops outside the swing phase, and turning the sound into an instruction to delay everything.

Trial / keep if: First compare video annotation with what the player believes they are doing. Then test whether the marker makes sequencing more repeatable and improves shots. Retain only if useful; later timing is not itself success. Do not use this event-triggered beep to tell the player when to initiate the same drop.

C9Compact returnPost-stroke feedback · separate drill

Rationale: Fast-serve redirection benefits from a different preparation and drop than a full rally swing. US Open 2025

Sound: F after a return when preparation height and backswing/drop excursion match the selected compact-return profile.

Observe: Ball trajectory, racket head/handle, hands and verified return phase; side view with sufficient flight visible. Ball-height estimates must refer to the intended interception region, not an arbitrary earlier frame.

Check that the grip transition is complete in the reference clips. Do not infer it solely from the hands occupying the right image position. Australian Open 2024

Suppress / pitfalls: Second-serve attacks, improvised blocked volleys, unobserved incoming flight, and rewarding a short swing that simply produces a weak short return.

Trial / keep if: Use a separate, predictable fast-feed/serve block and a defined deep return target. Keep if clean contacts and useful return depth improve. A smaller backswing without better returns is not enough. Do not carry its band into slow rally feeds.

C10Brake and recoverMovement marker · separate drill

Rationale: Clarke's Shelton analyses discuss outside-foot placement and recovery from wide backhands. Dallas, Montreal

Sound: M at the first sustained reversal of pelvis movement toward the agreed recovery position after an observed outside-foot plant and the strike. The beep marks recovery onset; it does not claim to measure force in the foot.

Observe: Hips, knees, ankles/feet, body identity, shot phase and court orientation from a rear-oblique full-body view. Foot contact is a visual estimate unless additional sensors establish it.

Suppress / pitfalls: Sliding foot confused with a fixed plant, hop/crossover misclassification, a short-ball approach where forward movement is intended, or retreat that compromises the strike. Earlier recovery can be a valid timing pattern; treat it as outside this initial post-strike drill rather than automatically incorrect.

Trial / keep if: Use comfortable controlled wide feeds with a second ball to a known location. Keep if recovery time and next-ball readiness improve while the first shot remains useful. This tests a movement choice, not a requirement to copy professional hard-court sliding.

How to test a cue

These are proposed pilot procedures, not validated sample sizes or evidence that auditory feedback works. Clarke's Changing Technique and Contextual Interference motivate checking actual change, retention and transfer; the beep protocol is our design.

  1. Choose the problem first. Select one cue, one meaning, one stroke profile and one measurable shot goal, such as a deep crosscourt ball. Keep the same external goal in silent comparison blocks. Record handedness, grip, feed height/pace, target, camera view and audio route. Collect the five accepted reference repetitions and contrasting problem examples.
  2. Validate the observation before live use. Label at least 20 positive examples and 20 negatives, separate from calibration clips. For M, the target is the specified event; for F, it is the completed reference pattern. Include correctly tracked backhands that fail the selected target among negatives, as well as other strokes and tracking failures. Measure missed/extra events and timestamp error. Count detector abstentions on manually annotatable positives as misses for end-to-end recall; reserve unscorable for genuinely ambiguous reference footage and report abstention rate separately. A provisional court-pilot gate is at least 90% precision and recall and no duplicate/wrong-person/stale-session sounds. These are engineering triage thresholds, not proof of coaching value.
  3. Start with 10 baseline, 10 cue-on, and 10 immediate cue-off repetitions. Keep feeds comparable; record every attempt, including misses. Limit a session to one or two candidates. A second matched session reverses on/off block order after baseline to expose simple warm-up effects. This is a screening experiment, not a causal study.
  4. Measure sound at the ear. Use synchronized motion recording and a microphone at the actual earphone/speaker output. Record event source time, decision time and audible onset separately. Report median and worst observed delay/jitter for each route. Before scoring, fix a cue-specific acceptable delay using annotated phase durations. If the sound arrives in the next movement phase or varies enough to confuse it, use post-stroke feedback or replay instead. “Move now” needs an earlier trigger plus demonstrated action lead time.
  5. Score mechanism and result separately. Have a coach review shuffled, muted clips for the chosen technical change. Also count in-court/target-zone shots, directional dispersion and mishits; record player distraction on a consistent 0–5 scale. Log detection failures separately from bad shots. Measure spin only with a method that actually estimates it.
  6. Check retention and transfer. At the next session, begin without cues. Then vary height/pace or direction and finally try normal rallying without sound. Keep a candidate for further testing only if the intended change persists, shot quality holds or improves, and distraction is acceptable to the player. A better-looking movement only during beeps is inconclusive.

For manual prototypes, a coach can provide post-stroke feedback. Human-triggered beeps cannot validate millisecond movement-marker timing. Timing candidates need annotated replay or an instrumented detector before live evaluation. Record “not tested” rather than presenting these proposals as available app functionality.
